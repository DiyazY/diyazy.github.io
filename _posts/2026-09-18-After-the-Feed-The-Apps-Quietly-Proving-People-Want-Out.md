[Part 2](/2026/09/18/After-the-Feed-How-the-Last-Three-Takeovers-Actually-Happened.html) ended on a claim: the opening for a new social platform is *authenticity*, and the overlooked capability is the phone's ability to prove something is real. That could stay a nice theory. Except it isn't only a theory — pieces of it are already shipping, making money, and keeping people around. They just don't look like "the next social network." They look like small, odd, single-purpose apps.

Put four of them side by side and they stop looking random.

<!--more-->

## Locket: the constraint *is* the product

[Locket](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/locket-widget/id1600525061) does one thing: it puts a photo from a close friend directly on your home screen, as a widget, no app to open. And it enforces a hard cap — you can only share with a small handful of people.

That cap is the whole idea. There's no follower count, no public feed, no algorithm deciding who sees what, no audience to perform for. By *removing* the things every other social app maximizes, Locket became genuinely intimate — and it worked commercially, racking up on the order of 80 million downloads and steady subscription revenue (Sensor Tower pegged a recent month around $300k), after raising $12.5M back in 2022.

The proof point: stripping the audience out of "social" isn't a compromise. For a lot of people it's the feature they were missing.

## Retro: people will pay *not* to be hooked

[Retro](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/retro-photos-with-friends/id6443709020), built by former Instagram engineers at Lone Palm Labs, is almost aggressively calm. It's a chronological photo journal you share only with friends, with weekly recaps instead of an endless feed. No ads. And it deliberately reintroduces friction that the industry spent fifteen years removing — you can mail an actual **physical postcard** of a photo from inside the app, for a couple of dollars.

Here's the part that should get any builder's attention: people pay for this. Retro runs on subscriptions — around **$36 a year** — and in 2026 it raised a **$21 million Series A** (led by Field Ventures and Thrive Capital) at a valuation north of $100 million, with roughly 1.7 million monthly users and in-app spending up more than 460% over half a year.

The proof point: a non-addictive, ad-free, deliberately slow product isn't charity. It's a fundable business, precisely *because* it refuses the feed model.

## Halide: the anti-AI camera

Your phone doesn't really take photos anymore; it computes them. Every shot is fused from multiple frames, denoised, sharpened, and smoothed by AI before you ever see it. [Halide's **Process Zero**](https://www.lux.camera/introducing-process-zero-for-iphone/) exists to turn all of that *off* — a single exposure, straight from the sensor, no computational photography, no neural retouching, delivered as a plain HEIF and a raw DNG. It even runs on non-Pro iPhones, and it's faster than Apple's own raw pipeline.

People buy a camera app whose entire selling point is *doing less* to the image. The photo looks like light hitting a sensor, not like a model's idea of a nicer version of your face.

The proof point: there's real, paying demand for **optical honesty** — images you can trust because the machine kept its hands off them. That's the authenticity wedge from Part 2, in shipping form.

## Instax: the object that can't be faked

Zoom out from apps entirely. Fujifilm's **Instax** instant cameras — physical film, a print you hold — quietly became one of the company's crown jewels, with the business hitting record sales around **¥150 billion** (over a billion dollars), led largely by Gen Z and millennial buyers. In an era of infinite, editable, AI-generated images, a chemical print that developed in front of you is the one photo nobody can quietly alter later.

The proof point: the appetite for **tangible, un-fakeable** artifacts is large, growing, and generational — not a nostalgia blip.

## What the four have in common

None of these is "the" replacement for the feed. Individually they're a widget, a journal, a camera mode, a toy. But read together, they're a map of the demand:

- **Constraint** beats infinity (Locket's cap, Retro's slowness).
- **Authenticity** beats polish (Halide's raw honesty).
- **Tangibility** beats ephemerality (Retro's postcards, Instax's prints).
- **Subscriptions and objects** beat advertising as a way to get paid.

Every one of them is profitable or well-funded doing the *opposite* of what the incumbents optimize. What none of them has done yet is put it all in one place, on shared infrastructure, at platform scale. That gap — the openings nobody has fully built — is the next part.
