## The realistic build order

Nobody ships all three openings at once, and they shouldn't try. The sane path is boring and sequential, and it follows the thread we've been pulling: **trust first, everything else after.**

1. **Do one thing perfectly: the honest camera.** Ship the capture-and-sign product on its own — a camera and a small network where photos are provably real. It's useful even to an audience of one, and it establishes the thing nobody else has.
2. **Open the protocol and borrow an audience.** Publish the schemas, run the indexer, and cross-post into the wider network so new users have somewhere to be discovered on day one. Solve cold-start by not launching on an island.
3. **Add the ambient layer and the physical rails.** Only once trust and distribution exist do you bolt on the glanceable close-circle widgets and the print-and-ship pipeline — the parts that turn a camera into a habit and a business.

Each stage is shippable and useful by itself. That's how you avoid the graveyard of grand platforms that needed everything working at once to be worth anything.

## Three honest futures

I don't know which of these happens. Speculative honesty means naming all three.

**The hopeful version.** Provenance becomes a quiet layer of the whole web — "verified real" is just a thing photos can be, the way "https" is just a thing links are. A handful of small, trusted, sustainable networks take the emotional center of people's lives back from the broadcast feeds, which shrink into what they always should have been: entertainment channels, clearly labeled. You still doomscroll sometimes. But the app where your actual friends live is a different, calmer place, and you can believe what you see in it.

**The grim version.** "Verified real" gets captured. Provenance turns into a gate — a premium tier, or worse, a de-facto identity mandate where unsigned content is treated as suspect and posting anything requires proving who and where you are. The tools built to restore trust become tools for surveillance and control, and the incumbents adopt just enough of the standard to lock the door behind them. Authenticity, weaponized.

**The boring-but-likely version.** No single winner, no clean revolution. Provenance shows up in some apps and not others. Group chats and close-friend apps keep quietly eating the feed's role without ever "beating" it. The big platforms lose their monopoly on our attention but not their existence. It's messier and slower than either the dream or the nightmare — a patchwork, trending gently in the right direction, most days.

My honest bet is the third, with pushes toward the first wherever someone builds well and the second wherever someone builds carelessly.

## What actually matters here

Strip away the specific products and protocols, and the durable claim is small: **the next social platform that matters won't win by having a better feed. It'll win by not being a feed at all** — by optimizing for believable human presence instead of time-on-app.

Whether that's a verified-camera network, a darkroom that mails you things, an ambient circle, or something none of us has pictured yet, the two ingredients are finally both here at once: a large, paying, generational appetite for authenticity, and the hardware and standards to actually deliver it. That combination didn't exist five years ago. It does now.

So the next time you open a *social* app and it hands you a stranger and a deep fryer, remember it doesn't have to be like that. It's like that because of a business model — and business models, as the last three takeovers showed, are exactly the thing that gets replaced.
